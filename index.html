    About this map

    This map is made for educational purposes only.

    The roster is a curated snapshot, not a complete census. Some researchers, labs and companies are certainly missing, and titles or affiliations may be out of date — people at this career stage move often. Lineage links are only drawn where a source stated them.

    State and country borders come from simplified public cartographic data (Natural Earth, 1:110m) and are drawn for illustration. They do not represent any official, legal or government position on boundaries, and no political claim is intended by any line or label.

    How to use this map

    A map of who works on DNA nanotechnology, where, and who taught whom. Every dot is an institution; open one to find the people, then follow a person to see their academic family tree drawn across the world.

    Five things to try

    1. Turn the globe. Drag it with one finger or the mouse. Scroll, pinch, or use + − on the left to zoom.
    2. Open an institution. Dots are sized by how many people are there. A dot with a ring holds several institutions too close to separate — click it and they fan out.
    3. Follow a person. Their card lists who trained them and whom they trained, and the arcs on the globe join those places. The globe stops turning so you can read.
    4. Look up a conference. Blue flags are upcoming, red ones already happened.
    5. See how the field was built. 44 milestones from 1982 to now, and the people behind them.

    Reading the map

    • Dot size — how many people and companies sit at that place.
    • Dot colour — the dominant research focus there. The key is in the filter panel.
    • A number inside — more than one entry; a pale centre means mixed fields.
    • A thin ring — several institutions stacked. Click to fan them out, or zoom in.
    • A hairline leader — the dot was nudged aside to stay readable; the line points at its true spot.
    • Arcs — who trained whom. The colours are explained bottom right.
    • Flags — conferences. Blue is still to come and waves; red already happened.

    Finding one person

    Type any name, lab, city or country into the search box in Find & filter, bottom left. Or narrow the map first with the Research focus and Country chips. The panel has − and ↕ buttons to shrink or expand it if it is in your way.

    The buttons, top right

    • Events — conferences and meet-ups, with live countdowns and links.
    • Breakthroughs — the history, with a timeline you can click and threads you can filter.
    • Both panels can be dragged by their title bar if they cover the globe.

    Handy to know

    • Esc closes whatever is open.
    • Reset, in the filter panel, puts everything back including the rotation.
    • Rotation stops when you pick a person. Switch it back on with Slow rotation.
    • Country names, state borders and the moon can all be turned off under Map.

    Breakthroughs — how DNA became a material

    How DNA stopped being something you read and became something you build with — 44 milestones across four decades, and the 10 fields the technique ended up in. 73 of the people credited here are on the globe: tap any name and the map will fly to them and open their lineage. Milestones up to 2017 follow the timeline and reference list of Seeman & Sleiman, Nature Reviews Materials 3, 17068 (2017); later ones follow Visions of DNA Nanotechnology at 40 (Jonoska & Winfree, eds., Springer 2023).

    The idea, and molecules to test it1982 – 1993

    Before any of this, DNA was something you read. These papers made it something you build with.

    1982
    A paper almost nobody read
    Seeman, J. Theor. Biol. 99, 237

    Proposed that branched DNA junctions joined by complementary sticky ends could self-assemble into designed three-dimensional lattices — scaffolds to hold other molecules still for crystallography. Every later result descends from this one argument.

    1983
    The first junction that would not move
    Kallenbach, Ma & Seeman, Nature 305, 829

    Natural Holliday junctions slide, because their two-fold symmetry lets the branch point migrate. Designing the four arms with unique sequences removed the symmetry and froze the branch in place. The first designed DNA object.

    1991
    A cube made of DNA
    Chen & Seeman, Nature 350, 631

    Three-arm junctions connected into a molecule with the connectivity of a cube — the first designed three-dimensional object built from nucleic acid.

    1991
    A knot tied in one strand
    Mueller, Du & Seeman, JACS 113, 6306

    A trefoil knot synthesised from a single strand, showing that DNA topology itself is programmable, not merely its shape.

    1993
    The double-crossover tile
    Fu & Seeman, Biochemistry 32, 3211

    Two helices held together by two strand exchanges rather than one. The extra crossover made the motif rigid enough to build extended structures with. Almost everything that follows stands on this tile.

    Proof that a molecule can compute1994 – 2000

    Two communities arrived at once: computer scientists who saw a parallel machine, and chemists who saw a construction material.

    1994
    A Hamiltonian path in a test tube
    Adleman, Science 266, 1021

    Each node of a seven-node graph became a 20-base oligonucleotide; hybridisation searched the solution space in parallel. Slow and unscalable, but it demonstrated computation at the molecular level and founded DNA computing.

    1996
    DNA as a builder of inorganic matter
    Alivisatos et al., Nature 382, 609  ·  Mirkin, Letsinger, Mucic & Storhoff, Nature 382, 607

    Two papers in one issue. One attached a single strand to a gold particle to make discrete assemblies; the other coated particles with a dense shell of DNA, which became spherical nucleic acids and the basis of colorimetric detection rivalling PCR for sensitivity.

    1998
    Crystals by design
    Winfree, Liu, Wenzler & Seeman, Nature 394, 539

    DX tiles assembled into predesigned periodic 2D lattices. Hairpins standing perpendicular to the plane acted as topographic markers under AFM — proof the pattern was programmed rather than accidental. The first time DNA's information was used to grow a designed crystal.

    1999
    The first DNA machine
    Mao, Sun, Shen & Seeman, Nature 397, 144

    Two DX motifs joined by a segment switched between right-handed B-DNA and left-handed Z-DNA by adding and removing cobalt hexammine, twisting the structure through repeated cycles and reported by FRET.

    2000
    Fuel made of DNA
    Yurke, Turberfield, Mills, Simmel & Neumann, Nature 406, 605

    The DNA tweezers: closed by a fuel strand, reopened by its complement. It introduced toehold-mediated strand displacement, in which a short unpaired overhang lets one strand peel another away. That single mechanism powers nearly all dynamic DNA nanotechnology since.

    2000
    Tiles that compute as they grow
    Mao, LaBean, Reif & Seeman, Nature 407, 493

    Triple-crossover tiles performing a logical operation during crystal growth — algorithmic self-assembly, where the structure is the output of a computation.

    Arbitrary shape2004 – 2006

    The moment the field stopped being difficult. Origami turned DNA construction into something a student could do in an afternoon.

    2004
    Sierpinski triangles
    Rothemund, Papadakis & Winfree, PLoS Biol. 2, e424

    Algorithmic assembly producing an aperiodic fractal — a computation made visible under the microscope, and an honest look at the error rates that still limited it.

    2004
    Rigid triangles from floppy junctions
    Liu, Wang, Deng, Walulu & Mao, JACS 126, 2324

    The tensegrity triangle: three helices held in mutual tension, rigid in all three directions. Five years later it was the motif that finally produced a macroscopic 3D crystal.

    2004
    RNA joins in
    Chworos et al., Science 306, 2068

    Programmable RNA tectosquares, opening RNA nanotechnology as a parallel discipline with its own folding rules and the advantage of being genetically encodable.

    2004
    Amplification without enzymes
    Dirks & Pierce, PNAS 101, 15275

    Hybridization chain reaction: metastable hairpins that stay closed until a trigger strand opens the first and starts a cascade. Now a standard tool in imaging and diagnostics.

    2004
    A scaffold folded by helpers
    Shih, Quispe & Joyce, Nature 427, 618

    A 1.7-kilobase single strand folded into an octahedron with the aid of five short strands — the direct ancestor of origami.

    2006
    DNA origami
    Rothemund, Nature 440, 297

    One long viral scaffold plus hundreds of short staples folds into essentially any 2D shape: smiley faces, stars, a map of the Americas. Roughly 200 individually addressable points in 100 × 100 nm, and the staples need not even be purified. The single largest inflection in the field's history.

    Three dimensions, motion, and matter2008 – 2012

    Having solved shape, the field went after rigidity, movement, and the organisation of things that are not DNA.

    2008
    Nanoparticle crystals grown by DNA
    Nykypanchuk, Maye, van der Lelie & Gang, Nature 451, 549  ·  Park et al., Nature 451, 553

    Colloidal crystallisation programmed by sequence, with lattice symmetry set by the DNA linkers rather than by the particles — “nanoparticle atoms joined by DNA bonds”.

    2009
    Origami in three dimensions
    Douglas et al., Nature 459, 414  ·  Dietz, Douglas & Shih, Science 325, 725

    Helices packed on a honeycomb lattice made rigid 3D origami; then deliberate insertion and deletion of bases introduced controlled twist and curvature. Shape became a continuous design variable.

    2009
    A box with a lid
    Andersen et al., Nature 459, 73

    Six origami sheets folded into a cube whose lid opens on a DNA key — the template for every cargo-carrying container that followed.

    2009
    The 1982 goal, reached
    Zheng et al., Nature 461, 74

    Tensegrity triangles tailed with two-base sticky ends associated in directions spanning 3D space and gave a self-assembled macroscopic DNA crystal. Twenty-seven years after the proposal that started the field.

    2009
    A bipedal Brownian motor
    Omabegho, Sha & Seeman, Science 324, 67

    A two-legged walker with coordinated gait moving directionally along a track.

    2010
    A molecular assembly line
    Gu, Chao, Xiao & Seeman, Nature 465, 202  ·  Lund et al., Nature 465, 206

    An origami track carrying three programmable cargo-donating machines and a walker that collects from each in turn; alongside it, molecular “spiders” following prescriptive landscapes.

    2011
    A pattern that copies itself
    Wang et al., Nature 478, 225

    Self-replication of information-bearing nanoscale patterns — non-living matter reproducing an arbitrary sequence of tiles.

    2012
    Bricks instead of a scaffold
    Wei, Dai & Yin, Nature 485, 623  ·  Ke, Ong, Shih & Yin, Science 338, 1177

    Hundreds of short, unique strands interlocking like LEGO, with no long scaffold at all. A molecular canvas: choose which bricks to include and you carve the shape, in 2D and then in 3D.

    2012
    Optics written in sequence
    Kuzyk et al., Nature 483, 311

    Gold nanoparticles arranged in left- and right-handed helices on origami, with circular dichroism spectra predicted in advance and then measured. Plasmonic properties designed at the sequence level.

    2012
    A logic-gated nanorobot
    Douglas, Bachelet & Church, Science 335, 831

    An origami barrel locked by two aptamer latches, opening only when it meets the right combination of cell-surface antigens and exposing its payload to manipulate signalling.

    2012
    DNA through a membrane
    Langecker et al., Science 338, 932

    A designed origami channel with a cholesterol belt, inserting into lipid bilayers and conducting ions — a synthetic nanopore with a programmable lumen.

    2012
    Motor proteins in tug of war
    Derr et al., Science 338, 662

    Origami used as a rigid chassis holding dynein and kinesin at chosen numbers, spacings and orientations, turning a structural tool into an instrument for cell biology.

    Into other people's fields2014 – 2017

    DNA nanotechnology stopped being a destination and became a technique other disciplines borrowed.

    2014
    RNA that folds as it is written
    Geary, Rothemund & Andersen, Science 345, 799

    Cotranscriptional folding: because transcription is slow relative to folding, the shape emerges as the strand is synthesised. That makes the structure genetically encodable, and so producible inside a cell.

    2014
    DNA-PAINT
    Jungmann et al., Nat. Methods 11, 313

    Transient binding of dye-labelled strands to docking sites produces blinking on demand, giving super-resolution imaging with programmable multiplexing. Now a mainstream microscopy method well outside this field.

    2014
    Casting metal in DNA moulds
    Sun et al., Science 346, 1258361

    Rigid origami boxes used as moulds inside which gold seeds grow into prescribed shapes — DNA as a foundry rather than a scaffold.

    2015
    Meshes, automatically
    Benson et al., Nature 523, 441

    Any polyhedral mesh converted into a scaffold routing by algorithm. Design stopped being craft and became software, and wireframe structures survive low-salt conditions that solid origami does not.

    2015
    Wires on a breadboard
    Knudsen et al., Nat. Nanotechnol. 10, 892

    Individual conjugated polymers routed along designed paths on an origami surface — a step toward organising optical and electronic molecular wires into arbitrary geometries.

    2016
    Printing recognition onto particles
    Edwardson, Lau, Bousmail, Serpell & Sleiman, Nat. Chem. 8, 162

    A DNA cage transfers an anisotropic pattern of strands onto a gold particle and is then reused. The nanostructure as a printing press rather than a permanent scaffold — a route past DNA's own fragility.

    2016
    Rewriting base pairing from outside
    Avakyan et al., Nat. Chem. 8, 368

    Cyanuric acid, a small molecule with three thymine-like faces, reprograms unmodified poly(adenine) into triple helices that assemble into long fibres. The assembly rules changed without touching the DNA.

    2017
    DNA folded by protein
    Praetorius & Dietz, Science 355, eaam5488

    TAL effector protein dimers bring chosen double-helix segments together, folding long double-stranded DNA into designed shapes. A genuine departure from Watson–Crick-directed assembly, and a route to genetically encoded structures.

    2017
    A device inside a crystal
    Hao et al., Nat. Chem. 9, 824

    A working nanomechanical device operating within a self-assembled 3D DNA crystal — the scaffold and the machine, finally in one object.

    Scale, and the clinic2018 – 2026

    The open question of the next decade is whether any of it leaves the laboratory. Two things have to hold: error rates low enough to trust, and structures that survive a bloodstream.

    2018
    A nanorobot that treats tumours
    Li et al., Nature Biotechnology, 2018

    Thrombin-loaded origami that unfolds at tumour-associated vasculature and induces local clotting, cutting off the blood supply. Tested in mice and in Bama miniature pigs, and reported as immunologically inert.

    2018
    Neural networks in a tube
    Cherry & Qian, Nature, 2018

    Winner-take-all strand-displacement circuits classifying handwritten digits — pattern recognition performed by molecules, with no hardware at all.

    2019
    Algorithms that run reliably
    Woods et al., Nature, 2019

    Twenty-one different algorithms executed by one reprogrammable tile set, with error rates finally low enough to be useful. The error problem that had limited algorithmic assembly since 2004 was, at last, largely tamed.

    2022
    An electrically driven rotary motor
    Pumm et al., Nature, 2022

    An origami rotor turned continuously by an alternating electric field. Directed, externally powered motion — an answer to the field's oldest complaint, that its machines run once and then stop at equilibrium.

    2023
    Origami T-cell engagers
    Wagenbauer et al., Nature Nanotechnology, 2023

    Logic-gated multispecific engagers that bring T cells to tumour cells only when the right antigen combination is present. Now the basis of a clinical-stage company in Munich.

    2026
    Twenty years of origami
    March 2026

    Two decades on from Rothemund's paper, the field marks the anniversary with a wave of retrospectives. Origami therapeutics are approaching trials, DNA data storage has an industry, and DNA-PAINT sits in microscopy facilities that have never heard of a tensegrity triangle.

    Where the technique ended up

    Structural biology and crystallography

    The original purpose. Lattices designed to hold guest molecules in fixed orientation so that diffraction can solve them — a way around the crystallisation bottleneck. Reached in 2009, and now extended to crystals hosting working devices and semiconductors.

    Molecular computation

    From Adleman's brute-force search to strand-displacement circuits, seesaw gates, oscillators and neural networks that recognise patterns. The field's persistent difficulty is that molecules are addressed chemically, not physically, which buys massive parallelism at the cost of speed and reuse.

    Nanoparticle assembly, plasmonics and metamaterials

    DNA as the bond between inorganic “atoms”: colloidal superlattices with sequence-set symmetry, chiral plasmonic helices with designed circular dichroism, nanoantennas that focus light into zeptolitre volumes, and moulds for casting metal.

    Machines, motors and robots

    Tweezers, walkers, spiders, assembly lines, pliers and rotary motors. The open problem, stated repeatedly across the field, is energy: hybridisation buys one move, and biology's machines run continuously on chemical fuel.

    Imaging and single-molecule measurement

    DNA-PAINT and hybridization chain reaction turned nanostructures into ordinary microscopy reagents. Origami frames also serve as observation stages for high-speed AFM, letting single DNA reactions be watched directly.

    Medicine, delivery and diagnostics

    Cages and barrels that open on a molecular cue, framework nucleic acids as biosensor scaffolds, siRNA carriers, aptamer-gated robots, tumour-targeting nanorobots and origami vaccines. Nuclease resistance, magnesium dependence and immunogenicity remain the hurdles.

    Membranes and synthetic cells

    Membrane-spanning channels, origami nanopores, lipid-templated liposomes, and structures patterned on supported bilayers by self-organising protein systems — DNA as a component of bottom-up synthetic cells.

    Bulk and hybrid materials

    Hydrogels that respond to pH and light, silicified origami that survives what bare DNA cannot, DNA-polymer conjugates, mechanical metamaterials, and scaffolds for guided biomineralisation in bone and enamel.

    Data storage and information

    DNA as an archival medium at petabytes per gram, with array synthesis and next-generation sequencing as the read and write heads. The one subfield that has grown a real industry, and the one most likely to pull the rest along with it.

    Mathematics

    A genuine new subfield: knot theory and graph invariants that existing mathematics could not supply, tile assembly theory, thermodynamics of molecular computation, and the coarse-grained models (oxDNA, CanDo, DAEDALUS) that made design predictive.
